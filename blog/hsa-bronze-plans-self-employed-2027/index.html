HSA Bronze Plans in 2027: A Guide for Self-Employed Families

By Ally Valdez November 3, 2026 6 min read

If you work for yourself, you pay for your own health insurance and you feel every premium increase. Open Enrollment for 2027 is now open, and there is one newer option many self-employed families have not heard about: every Marketplace Bronze and Catastrophic plan now works with a Health Savings Account (HSA).

That was not always the case. Before 2026, only certain high-deductible plans qualified, and many Bronze plans did not. Here is what changed, how much you can set aside for 2027, and when a Bronze plan with an HSA may or may not make sense for your family.

What Changed for Bronze Plans?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed the HSA rules. Under IRS Notice 2026-5, Bronze and Catastrophic plans available through a Marketplace count as HSA-eligible plans for months starting January 1, 2026. They qualify even if they do not meet the usual high-deductible plan limits.

A plan bought directly from an insurance company can count too, as long as the same plan is available on the Marketplace. Small-employer SHOP coverage generally does not get this treatment. CMS says that in 2027, eligible HealthCare.gov shoppers will have an average of 35 HSA-eligible plans to choose from.

How Does an HSA Work?

An HSA is a savings account you own, and it stays with you if you change plans or close your business. Under IRS rules, the money can be used for qualified medical expenses, and the balance carries over from year to year. There is no use-it-or-lose-it deadline.

Your contributions are generally deductible, which is why people who are self-employed pay attention to it. Please ask your tax professional how this applies to your own return.

Can an HSA Help You Stay Under the Subsidy Cliff?

It may. For 2027 coverage, premium tax credits stop at 400% of the Federal Poverty Level. That is $63,840 for one person and $132,000 for a family of four. Self-employed income can land close to that line, and an HSA contribution lowers the income figure the Marketplace uses.

Here is a simple illustration. A single freelancer with $65,000 of income for ACA purposes is over the line. A $4,500 HSA contribution would bring that figure to $60,500. A family of four at $135,000 that contributes $9,000 would land at $126,000.

Your real number depends on your whole tax picture, including your self-employment deductions. And because there is no longer a cap on repaying excess advance tax credits, an income estimate that is too low can turn into a tax bill. Your tax professional can help you run the numbers. Our 2027 ACA Marketplace Guide has the full income chart.

Who Can Contribute?

Having a Bronze plan is step one. Under IRS rules, you also need to meet these conditions:

  • No other disqualifying health coverage. A spouse's regular health plan, for example, may keep you from contributing. Dental, vision, and accident coverage are generally allowed.
  • Not enrolled in Medicare. If you are nearing 65, plan ahead for when your contributions need to stop.
  • Not claimed as a dependent on someone else's tax return.

Already on a 2026 Bronze plan?

You may still be able to contribute for 2026. The 2026 limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, and IRS rules generally allow contributions up to the April tax filing deadline. Contributions for 2027 can generally be made through the April 2028 deadline.

Who Should Think Twice?

A lower premium is only part of the story. Bronze plans usually have high deductibles, and KFF reports the average Marketplace deductible across all metal levels reached $3,786 in 2026. Before you choose Bronze, it helps to think through a few things:

  • You expect regular care. If you see specialists, take costly prescriptions, or are planning a pregnancy, a higher metal level may cost less over the full year.
  • You qualify for cost-sharing reductions. Those extra savings only apply to Silver plans. If your income is at or below 250% of the poverty level, compare Silver first.
  • You cannot fund the account. An empty HSA does not help with the deductible. Keep some cash available for what you might owe.
  • You want the HSA to pay your premiums. In most cases it cannot. Marketplace premiums are generally not a qualified HSA expense.

Keep your receipts

Money taken out of an HSA for something other than qualified medical expenses is taxable and generally comes with an additional 20% tax. That extra tax does not apply after age 65, though income tax on non-medical withdrawals still does.

What About Catastrophic Plans?

Catastrophic plans count as HSA-eligible as well, but not everyone can buy one. They are open to people under 30, and federal guidance also allows a hardship exemption for some people who are not eligible for premium tax credits or cost-sharing reductions based on income. Premium tax credits generally cannot be used on these plans. An agent can check whether you qualify.

Your 2027 Enrollment Dates

On HealthCare.gov, Open Enrollment runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027. Choose a plan by December 15 for coverage that starts January 1. State-run Marketplaces can set different dates, so please check yours.

If you are only looking for a low-cost bridge for a few months, our short-term health insurance guide explains how that differs from a Marketplace plan. Short-term plans are not Bronze plans and do not carry the same protections.

The Bottom Line

A Bronze plan paired with an HSA can be a useful fit for a healthy, self-employed family with some savings, especially one near the subsidy cliff. It may not be the right choice for a family that expects steady medical bills. The way to find out is to compare the full year's cost, not just the monthly premium.
