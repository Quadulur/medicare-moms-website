Short-Term Health Insurance

Short-Term Health Insurance in 2027: Who It Fits, Who Should Skip It

By Ally Valdez October 19, 2026 8 min read

ACA premiums are going up again for 2027, with a median proposed increase of about 15% on top of last year's jump. The enhanced subsidies are still gone, so anyone who earns over the subsidy cliff pays full price. That is why more families are asking us the same question: "What about a short-term plan?"

For the right person, a short-term health plan is a low-cost way to bridge a gap in coverage. For the wrong person, it can leave them with a large medical bill and no way to get real coverage until next year. Here is how to tell which one you are.

What Is Short-Term Health Insurance?

Short-term health insurance (the official name is short-term, limited-duration insurance) is temporary medical coverage that is not an ACA plan. Because it does not have to follow ACA rules, it works very differently:

  • Health questions decide if you can get it. The insurer can turn you down or charge more based on your medical history.
  • Pre-existing conditions are usually excluded. Most plans look back at your recent health history and will not pay for conditions you were diagnosed with or treated for during that period.
  • Fewer required benefits. Maternity care, mental health, and prescriptions are often limited or not covered.
  • A cap on what the plan pays. Plans set a maximum benefit, such as $250,000 to several million dollars, instead of the ACA's unlimited coverage.
  • You can buy it any time of year, and coverage can start as soon as the next day.
  • No premium tax credits. You pay the full premium, but it is often much lower than a full-price ACA plan for a healthy person.

How Long Can a Short-Term Plan Last?

The answer has changed several times. A 2024 federal rule limited short-term plans to 3 months, plus a 1-month extension, for a total of 4 months. In August 2025, the federal government said it would not prioritize enforcing that limit while it works on a new rule, and invited states to take the same approach. As of this writing, we have not seen a new final rule published.

In practice, the length you can buy depends on your state and the insurance company. For example, Allstate Health Solutions currently offers short-term medical plans with terms from 30 days to 3 months. Some states set their own limits, and some, including California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, and New York, do not allow short-term plans at all. A licensed agent can tell you exactly what is available where you live.

Who a Short-Term Plan Can Work For

Short-term coverage works best as a bridge: a few healthy months between one real plan and the next. It is usually a good fit if you are:

  • Between jobs and waiting for new employer coverage to start. Many employers have a waiting period of up to 90 days.
  • Waiting for a new plan to begin. For example, you enroll in an ACA plan in December that starts January 1 and need something for the weeks before.
  • Healthy, self-employed, and over the subsidy cliff. For 2027, a single person earning more than $63,840, or a family of four earning more than $132,000, gets no help with ACA premiums. If you have no ongoing health conditions, a short-term plan can cost far less for the months you need it.
  • A young adult coming off a parent's plan at 26 who needs coverage until a job or ACA plan starts.
  • Retiring a few months before 65 and healthy enough to qualify, needing coverage only until Medicare begins.

Who Should Skip a Short-Term Plan

  • Anyone with an ongoing health condition, such as diabetes, heart disease, a cancer history, or anything you see a doctor for regularly. Those costs will likely not be covered.
  • Anyone who is pregnant or planning to be. Maternity care is usually excluded.
  • Anyone on regular prescriptions, especially brand-name or specialty drugs.
  • Anyone who qualifies for a subsidy. If your income is under the cliff, an ACA plan with a premium tax credit, and possibly lower deductibles, may cost about the same or less and covers far more.
  • Anyone on Medicare. Short-term plans are not sold to people with Medicare, and they are not a substitute for a Medicare Supplement or Medicare Advantage plan. For Medicare options, see our Medicare guide.

The trap most people do not know about

When a short-term plan ends, you do not get a special enrollment period for an ACA plan. Losing short-term coverage does not count as losing qualifying coverage. If your short-term plan ends in April and you get sick, you may have to wait until the next Open Enrollment (November 1) to get a plan that covers you. Always plan how you will get back into full coverage before your short-term plan ends.

Smart Ways to Use Short-Term Coverage

  • Use it as a bridge, not a long-term plan. Know the date your next real coverage starts.
  • Line it up with Open Enrollment. ACA Open Enrollment for 2027 runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027. Choose a plan by December 15 for coverage that starts January 1.
  • Read the pre-existing condition section closely. Ask how far back the plan looks and what it excludes.
  • Answer the health questions honestly. Wrong answers can lead to denied claims or a cancelled policy later.
  • Fill the gaps if needed. Accident, critical illness, and dental plans can be added for extra protection. Many carriers, including Allstate Health Solutions, offer these alongside short-term medical.

The Bottom Line

A short-term plan is not a cheaper version of an ACA plan. It is a different product with a specific job: covering a healthy person for a short gap. With 2027 ACA premiums rising and no subsidies above the cliff, it can save a lot of money for the right family. For anyone with health conditions, a baby on the way, or regular prescriptions, an ACA plan is still the safer choice. The best move is to compare both side by side before you buy.
